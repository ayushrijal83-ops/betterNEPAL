## Provenance and licensing

Every file here originates from **Unsplash**. The photographer name and photo
ID below are read from the original filenames, which Unsplash generates in the
form `<photographer-slug>-<photoId>-unsplash.jpg`. Each photo's own page is at
`https://unsplash.com/photos/<photoId>`.

> **Verify before any public launch.** The Unsplash License broadly permits
> commercial and non-commercial use without permission, and asks for — but does
> not require — attribution. It does **not** permit selling unaltered copies, nor
> compiling photos to build a competing service. Two further limits matter for a
> civic platform: the licence covers the *photograph*, not the **people, logos or
> trademarks** shown in it, and several of these frames contain identifiable
> members of the public. Confirm each photo's current licence on its Unsplash page
> and take your own view on depicting identifiable people before going live.

Attribution strings for a credits page are in the table below. No licence text
has been invented here: only what the filenames record and the standard Unsplash
terms are stated.

## Pipeline

Originals are **not** served. Each image is resized to the sizes it is actually
displayed at and encoded twice — WebP plus a JPEG fallback — with quality driven
down to a per-role byte budget. Images that resist compression (dense foliage)
get fewer pixels rather than heavier artefacts.

## Gap: no civic-problem photography

This set is landscape, heritage and travel photography. It contains **no images
of the problems the platform exists to record** — no damaged road surface, no
landslide scar, no uncollected waste, no failed drainage or downed line.

Those category cards therefore keep their icon treatment. Captioning a Himalayan
panorama “Landslide & flood” would misrepresent the platform's own subject matter
and push the site toward the tourism-brochure look the design brief rules out.

To photograph the problem cards properly, the set still needs: road surface
damage; a landslide or washout; uncollected waste or an overflowing site;
water infrastructure (leaking main, dry standpipe); electrical faults; and
damaged public buildings. Field documentary, ideally wide or 4:3.
